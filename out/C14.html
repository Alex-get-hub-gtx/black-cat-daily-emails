Living together without losing yourself

Three pillars have to stay standing.

Moving in is where most women lose the thing they built, and it happens so gradually that nobody notices until it's done.

Here's the mechanism.

Shared living creates efficiency, and efficiency quietly eliminates everything that was separately yours.

It's rational at every step. Why keep two of everything. Why go out separately when you could stay in together. Why keep your own place when you're always here. Why do your own thing on a Thursday when he's home.

Each decision is sensible. The sum of them is a woman with no independent life, achieved in about eighteen months, with no villain anywhere in the story.

Three pillars have to stay standing, and they're the three that go first.

Social. The standing night. Whatever it is, it survives the move. This is the one that goes first and the one that matters most, because it's your only source of outside perspective on the relationship you're now inside of.

Financial. Your own account and your own income, whatever the household arrangement is. Living together often produces a merge, and merges are very hard to unwind, and the woman in the merge is usually the one who loses the exit.

Something that's yours. The class, the project, the sport. It will be the first thing to get squeezed, because it's the easiest to move, and nobody will ever ask you to stop doing it.

Here's the practical version, and this matters more than the principle.

Decide these before you move, not after. Once you're in, every one of them becomes a negotiation, and you'll be negotiating from inside a situation where saying no looks like rejecting him.

Keep a room or a corner that's yours, literally. Even a desk. The psychological effect of a physical space that belongs to you alone is out of all proportion to its size.

Keep your name on something. A lease, an account, a membership, anything.

And the one nobody does: keep one night a week where you're out and he's home. Not a row, not a crisis. A Thursday. It does more for the relationship than any amount of togetherness, for all the reasons I've written about distance.

What actually works.

Write the three down before the move, and treat them as conditions rather than preferences.

Watch the social one specifically. If you've seen your friends less every month for four months, that's the signal, and it's always the first one.

And don't give up your place until you've lived together for a year, if you can possibly afford not to.

Nobody will ask you to give these up. You'll hand them over one sensible decision at a time, and notice about two years later.

Your one move today.

If you live together, score the three. Social, financial, yours.

Whichever is lowest, put one thing back this week.

Which went first for you? Reply, it's almost always the friends.

See you tomorrow!
