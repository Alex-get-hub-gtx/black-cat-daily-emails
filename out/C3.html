Your mother's marriage is in your body

You learned it before you had words.

You've said it before, probably with some force. I'm nothing like her. I'd never accept what she accepted.

And you might be right about the choices. You're almost certainly wrong about the nervous system, and that's where it lives.

Here's the mechanism.

The first relationship you ever studied, you studied before you could speak. And what you learned was not a set of opinions. It was a set of expectations about how love behaves.

Children are extraordinary observers of relational dynamics and terrible at interpreting them. So you absorbed the shape without the explanation. Who gets to be angry. Who manages whose mood. What happens right before someone goes quiet. Whether affection is reliable or has to be earned. How a woman behaves when she's disappointed and has decided not to say so.

All of it went in as procedure, not as belief. Which is why arguing with it does nothing.

And here's the part that catches women who've done a lot of work on themselves.

You can consciously reject every single thing about her marriage and still reproduce its dynamics exactly, because you're rejecting it at the level of opinion and running it at the level of reflex.

The most common version: a mother who kept the peace, and a daughter who swore she never would, who has spent fifteen years with men she manages carefully while telling herself she's nothing like her mother.

Here's how to find yours. Three questions, and answer them about the house, not about the people.

What happened in that house when someone was angry?

What did a woman have to do there to be loved?

And what was never said out loud?

Those three answers describe the template you're running. Not the one you believe in. The one your body executes under pressure.

What actually works at this level.

Separate the opinion from the reflex. You have both and they may be opposites. The reflex is what shows up at 11pm when he hasn't replied.

Watch for it in the small moments, because that's where it lives. The split second before you smooth something over. The automatic apology. The thing you decide not to mention.

And give yourself a different response for one specific situation, rather than trying to change the whole pattern. "When I feel the urge to keep the peace, I'm going to say the thing instead." One situation, practised repeatedly, rewires more than any amount of understanding.

You are not repeating her choices. You're running her procedure, and the procedure went in before you had any language to argue with it.

Your one move today.

Answer the three questions. Write them down, honestly, about the house you grew up in.

Then read the third answer twice.

What was never said out loud in your house? Reply, if you want to. These are the ones I hold most carefully.

See you tomorrow!
