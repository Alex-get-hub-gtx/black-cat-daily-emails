The two year rule

What six months hides.

Don't make a permanent decision about a person inside two years.

Not because love takes two years. Because certain things cannot be seen in less, and they're the things that determine the next thirty.

Here's what two years reveals that six months cannot.

A full cycle of difficulty. In two years, something will go wrong. A job, a death, an illness, money. You cannot know a person until you've seen them in trouble, and you cannot schedule trouble.

How he handles your difficulty, which is different and more important. A man can be excellent in his own crisis and useless in yours. The first time you need something substantial is the most informative day in the relationship, and it rarely happens in the first year.

Whether the initial presentation holds. Everyone can sustain a version of themselves for a while. Eighteen months is roughly where effortful behaviour becomes unsustainable and the actual settings reappear. This is also, not coincidentally, when a lot of relationships suddenly change character.

Repair. Not whether you argue, everyone argues. Whether you come back from it, how long it takes, and whether anything is different afterwards. Repair capacity is probably the best single predictor of whether a relationship survives, and you need several real ruptures to assess it.

And seasons. How he is in winter, under pressure at work, at Christmas with his family, on the third holiday rather than the first.

Here's why the rule matters specifically for you, now.

Time pressure argues against it. If you're thirty six and you've just met someone good, two years feels like an unaffordable luxury, and that's precisely when people commit at eight months and find out at three years.

I'm not going to pretend the maths isn't hard. But the alternative is deciding with a quarter of the information, and the cost of being wrong at that level is much higher than two years.

What you can do is use the time rather than just pass it. Most people spend the first two years being delighted. You can spend them watching, deliberately, at the moments that count.

What actually works.

Pay attention at the first real difficulty, whenever it comes. That day is worth six ordinary months.

Watch repair specifically. After an argument: how long, who moves, does anything change.

Don't mistake the eighteen month shift for the relationship failing. It's often just the presentation ending, and what's underneath may be fine. But look at it honestly.

And if you're deciding at eight months because of the clock, say that out loud to yourself first.

Six months tells you who he is when things are good. Two years tells you who he is, and only one of those is the man you'd be living with.

Your one move today.

Where are you in the timeline? And have you seen him in real trouble yet?

If the answer is no, you don't have the information yet, however good it feels.

Have you ever been surprised at eighteen months? Reply, almost everyone has.
